Most organisations have an incident response plan. Far fewer have tested whether their executives can actually use it under pressure. Some years ago a global insurance company asked me to test exactly that, and it was the first time the consultancy I worked for had done this kind of work. It went on to become a strong service line, delivered to more than ten organisations in its second year.

## How the exercises worked

Each exercise had four stages:

1. **Scenario development with the client.** I built a set of flexible scenarios that could be steered in the room depending on the decisions the executives made.
2. **Running the exercise.** The executive team worked through the scenario in real time while I introduced new information and pressure.
3. **Assessing the response** against two benchmarks: the client's own incident response procedures and good practice based on ISO/IEC 27035.
4. **Reporting back** in writing and in person, with specific areas for improvement.

Once the insurer had implemented its improvements, I ran a second set of scenarios to test the updated processes. Repeating the exercise is what turns it from a one-off event into a measurable improvement.

## Focus on people and process, not technology

Executive exercises aren't technical tests. The questions that matter are:

- Does everyone know what they need to do?
- Does everyone know what they are *authorised* to do?
- Do the documented processes actually work when people follow them?

## People usually have more authority than they think

The most consistent finding was that people in an incident believe they have less authority than they do. They wait for permission that they don't need, and that hesitation costs time. A large part of the value of these exercises is showing people, before a real incident, which decisions are already theirs to make.
